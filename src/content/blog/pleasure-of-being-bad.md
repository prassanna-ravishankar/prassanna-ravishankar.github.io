Coding agents are getting incredibly good. I give them something to work on, and more and more of the work actually gets done. There are still mistakes, still things to check, but I like being able to build this way. Projects that would once have stayed in a notebook can become things I use.

I wrote [last year](/blog/reflections-claude-code/) about how much I was shipping, and how strange it felt to be writing less of the code myself. There was excitement in that. I could follow an idea further before running out of time or getting stuck on something I didn't know how to do.

Alongside all of this, other things have been moving at a rather different pace.

Between October 2024 and September 2025, I lost 38 kilograms. A year of working on my fitness. Written down like that, it fits into two sentences. The dates sit next to each other, with the result conveniently attached. There was a whole year between them.

I don't want to turn that year into advice about discipline. It belongs here because of its duration. Some changes require you to keep participating while they happen. You can have help, and you can know what you're trying to do, but there is still the time you spend doing it.

Earlier this year, I started learning the drums.

I was shit for about four weeks. Then I started getting the rhythm. After that I plateaued again, and eventually broke through again. Now I can play basic grooves.

That's about where I am. There is plenty I still can't play. The progress has been uneven, with stretches where I was stuck between one thing beginning to work and the next thing making sense. Getting through the first four weeks didn't put an end to being a beginner.

I like having this alongside the things that are getting faster. It gives me somewhere to be a person learning something, with the fairly ordinary limitation that I can't do it yet.

There is a little awkwardness in the title of this piece. I wanted to get better at the drums. I did get better, and I'm pleased that I can now play something I couldn't play before. I wouldn't pretend that being stuck is always enjoyable. But I also don't want to write off those first weeks as time I should somehow have found a way to skip.

The beginning of an activity can ask for quite a lot before it gives much back. You are paying attention to things that will eventually become familiar. There is effort in getting started, and then more effort in staying with it long enough for something to begin working.

This is where habit seems useful to me. Returning gives an activity another chance. You don't need to reach a fresh conclusion about its value every time you sit down to do it. Sometimes you can just do a little more of it. There is room for an unremarkable attempt, without having to decide whether it says anything about your potential.

With an agent available, the first difficult moment in a task offers an easy place to hand it over. Often I should. There are plenty of things I want finished, and I have no particular attachment to the experience of finishing them myself. But I wonder about making that handoff automatic, before I've had time to find out whether I want to stay with the problem.

It's possible to leave very early. Before the details become interesting. Before the effort has had a chance to turn into involvement. I don't think I can always tell, at the beginning, which kind of afternoon I'm about to have.

![Loose teal and amber rhythms overlap, then open into spacious curves against a dark, textured background.](/images/blog/pleasure-of-being-bad/flow.webp)

Of course, help can also be what lets me stay.

That's part of what I enjoyed about building with agents in the first place. Having help with individual tasks let me spend longer with an idea. I could see more of it take shape, make changes, and keep going. I was involved, even though I wasn't doing every part myself.

There is something of a symphony in that possibility. Individual parts can be taken care of while you pay attention to how they fit together. You can follow the larger thing for a while, hear where it feels wrong, and work on it without continually having to stop. Automation can make space for that kind of flow.

I would like some of the time it frees to remain spacious. It doesn't all have to become capacity for another task. There can be room for learning something slowly, or for spending longer on a part of the work simply because it has become interesting.

I don't have a consistent rule for which effort to keep. I want the help with coding. I also want to learn to play the drums. Those seem quite comfortable together when I stop asking every activity to account for itself in the same way.

For now, I can play basic grooves. There is more to learn, and I can let that take its time. I can spend a little while with what I can already play, too.

The pleasure of being bad.

The pleasure of being human.
